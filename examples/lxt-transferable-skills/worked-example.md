# Worked Example: LxT Transferable Skills Worksheet
## For Learner-Centric MOOC Design

### About This Example

This is a filled-in copy of the
[LxT Transferable Skills Worksheet](../../templates/worksheets/lxt-transferable-skills-worksheet.md).
It uses an **Environmental Studies** course because most college faculty know
its basic ideas, whatever their own subject.

- **Core path:** one complete trajectory that targets **Systems Thinking**
- **Stretch path:** one more trajectory, sketched, that targets **Problem
  Decomposition**
- **Snapshots:** the same skill (Problem Decomposition) applied in three very
  different subjects, to show how the approach transfers

Use it as a model, not a script. Your own trajectory will look different.

**Note on resources:** The resources below are well-known, freely accessible
examples. Check each link and its license before using it in a course, as
online resources move or change.

---

### Part 1: Course and Module Context

1. Course Title: Environmental Studies (compulsory undergraduate course)

2. Module or LeD chosen: Module 2 - Ecosystems

3. Learning objectives of this module:
   1. Describe the structure and function of an ecosystem
   2. Explain food chains, food webs and energy flow
   3. Explain how ecosystems respond to disturbance

4. [Stretch] Other modules for an LxT later: Module 4 - Pollution;
   Module 6 - Environmental Policy

### Part 2: Looking Beyond the Content

1. A graduate three years from now is working in local government. A new road
   is proposed through a wetland near town.

2. Content alone will not prepare them for this: they may know what a wetland
   is, but not how to foresee what else the road will change - flooding,
   groundwater, bird life, fishing livelihoods.

3. The way of thinking that would help most: seeing connections and knock-on
   effects before deciding.

### Part 3: Choose a Transferable Skill

1. Skill chosen:
   - [x] Systems Thinking

2. Where it already shows up in the subject: Ecologists use it every time they
   explain how a change in one species affects the whole food web.

3. In plain words for learners: "Seeing how things are connected, so you can
   predict what else will change when one thing changes."

4. [Stretch] Skill map across modules:

| Module | Skill 1: Systems Thinking | Skill 2: Problem Decomposition |
|--------|---------------------------|--------------------------------|
| 2 - Ecosystems | Food webs and trophic cascades | - |
| 4 - Pollution | Pollutant pathways through air, water and soil | Breaking down a local pollution problem |
| 6 - Environmental Policy | Side effects of a policy | Turning a policy goal into action steps |

### Part 4: Identify Trajectories

#### Step A.1: Learner categories

- Learners without a biology background since school
- Learners who want to go deeper into ecology
- Working professionals interested in practical applications
- Learners preparing for competitive examinations
- Learners from rural areas with first-hand knowledge of local ecosystems

#### Step A.3: Prioritized categories and assigned skill

| Module | Learner Category | Learner Purpose | Priority | Target Skill | Expected Starting Level |
|--------|------------------|-----------------|----------|--------------|-------------------------|
| 2 | Learners who want to go deeper | Understand how ecosystems respond to change | 1 (built today - Core) | Systems Thinking | Emerging |
| 2 | Working professionals | Apply ideas to real decisions at work | 2 (Stretch) | Problem Decomposition | Developing |
| 2 | Learners without a biology background | Catch up on basic terms | 3 (later) | Self-Directed Learning | Emerging |

### Part 5: Identify Resources

#### Step B.1: Resources

| Module | Learning Trajectory | Resource URL | Media Type of Resource | Skill visible? |
|--------|---------------------|--------------|------------------------|----------------|
| 2 | Going deeper | "How Wolves Change Rivers" (Sustainable Human), https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ysa5OBhXz-Q | Video, about 4.5 min | Yes - it traces a chain of effects from one species to the shape of rivers |
| 2 | Going deeper | "Leverage Points: Places to Intervene in a System" by Donella Meadows, https://donellameadows.org/archives/leverage-points-places-to-intervene-in-a-system/ | Essay (web page) | Yes - an expert explains where small changes have large effects in any system |

### Part 6: Create Tasks with a Skill Lens

| Module | LxT Resource | Most Relevant portion (and why) | Task for learner | Skill move required |
|--------|--------------|---------------------------------|------------------|---------------------|
| 2 | How Wolves Change Rivers | Whole video (short). It shows a chain of effects from wolves to rivers, which is a clear trophic cascade | 1. Watch the video and draw a connection map with at least 6 elements (for example: wolves, deer, trees, birds, beavers, river banks). 2. Draw arrows to show what affects what. 3. Mark one place where an effect feeds back to strengthen its own cause. 4. Predict: if wolves were removed again, what would change first, and what would change last? | Traces cause-and-effect chains, including feedback loops; predicts delayed effects |
| 2 | Leverage Points | Introduction and the list of 12 leverage points only. It gives a simple, general way to find where to act in any system | Read the list. Look at your connection map from the video. Mark one place where a small change could have a large effect, and say which leverage point it matches | Identifies where a small change could have a large effect |

Task check:
- [x] Can a learner complete it without using the skill? No - the map and the
      prediction both require tracing connections.
- [x] Fits an Emerging starting level? Yes - the video gives the elements; the
      learner only has to connect them.
- [x] Reasonable time? About 30 minutes in total.

[Stretch] Version for a Developing learner: "Without listing the elements for
you, map the connections in a wetland near your home. Then predict what a new
road through it would change."

### Part 7: Assimilation Quiz and Skill Reflection

| LxT Resource | Task for the learner | Assimilation Quiz |
|--------------|----------------------|-------------------|
| How Wolves Change Rivers | Connection map and prediction | 1. According to the video, which animal was reintroduced to Yellowstone National Park? (a) Bears (b) Wolves (c) Elk (d) Beavers |
| | | 2. According to the video, why did deer start avoiding some valleys and gorges? |
| | | 3. Name one change to the rivers that the video describes. |
| Leverage Points | Mark a leverage point on the map | 1. In your own words, what does Meadows mean by a "leverage point"? |
| | | 2. According to Meadows, which is the more powerful place to intervene: changing a number (parameter) in a system, or changing the system's goal? |

Note that these questions only check that learners watched and read the
resources. None of them assess systems thinking - the task does that.

[Stretch] Skill reflection prompt: "When you started your connection map, where
did you begin - with the wolves or somewhere else? What made it easier to see
the connections?"

### Part 8: Connecting to Other LCM Elements

- LeD: In the next LeD on energy flow, add a reflection spot: "If the number of
  top predators halves, what happens two levels down?"
- LbD: An Apply-level question asking learners to predict the effect of
  removing one species from a given food web.
- LxI: A discussion where learners post their connection maps and compare
  where they placed feedback loops. **Stretch extension:** share a short
  article noting that scientists still debate how strong the Yellowstone
  effect was, and ask learners what their maps might have left out.

### Part 9: Quality Checklist

From the LxT Constructor:
- [ ] Minimum of 3 categories of learners - *3 identified; 1 built today,
      1 sketched, 1 planned*
- [ ] At least one resource for each cohort - *still to do for the third
      cohort*
- [x] Resources are free/open
- [x] Assimilation quiz checks that learners accessed the content

For the skill layer:
- [x] Skill named to learners in plain words
- [x] Task requires the skill move, not just recall
- [x] Resource shows the skill being used
- [x] Assimilation quiz is still a simple check of access
- [x] Reflection prompt asks how the learner worked
- [x] Task fits the time available

### Part 10: Peer Review

Reviewed by: a colleague from the Commerce department

| Reviewer prompt | Feedback |
|-----------------|----------|
| Two things that work well | 1. The connection map makes the skill visible on paper. 2. The prediction question pushes learners past just describing the video. |
| One thing to improve | Give learners an example of what a feedback loop looks like - "feeds back to its cause" may be new to them. |
| Could you follow the skill move without knowing this subject? | Yes - I could do the same with prices and demand in my subject. |
| Does the task really need the skill? | Yes. |

### Stretch Path: A Second Trajectory (Sketch)

| Field | Entry |
|-------|-------|
| Learner category | Working professionals |
| Target skill | Problem Decomposition (Developing) |
| Resource | UNEP, "Single-use Plastics: A Roadmap for Sustainability" (2018), https://www.unep.org/resources/report/single-use-plastics-roadmap-sustainability |
| Most relevant portion | The section setting out the steps recommended for policymakers |
| Task | Before reading, break "Reduce single-use plastic on our campus" into 4-6 sub-problems and order them. Then read the roadmap's steps. Which sub-problems did you miss? Which steps would you reorder for a campus? |
| Skill move | Lists sub-problems; identifies which depend on others; recombines and checks |
| Assimilation quiz | 1. Name two steps the roadmap recommends. 2. According to the report, why do bans alone sometimes fail? |

### Snapshots: Same Skill, Different Subjects

Each snapshot shows one row of Part 6 for **Problem Decomposition** in a
different subject. The skill move stays the same; only the content changes.

| Subject | LxT Resource | Task for learner | Skill move required |
|---------|--------------|------------------|---------------------|
| Data Structures | A recorded walkthrough of an expert designing a library book search system | Before watching, list the sub-problems in building the search (storing books, adding new ones, searching by title or author). After watching, compare your list with the expert's and note which sub-problem they solved first, and why | Lists sub-problems; identifies dependencies and order |
| History | A short open-access article on why a historical independence movement succeeded | Break "Why did the movement succeed?" into 4 smaller questions. Use the article to answer each one in two lines, then say which factor mattered most | Splits a big question into parts; recombines into one conclusion |
| Financial Accounting | A worked example of a small business's monthly cash budget | Break "Will this business run out of cash in the next 3 months?" into the figures you would need to find. Which figure would you check first? | Separates what is known from what must be found; orders the parts |

The same task starter from the catalog - "Break `[a problem in your subject]`
into smaller questions. Which one must be answered first, and why?" - works in
all three.
