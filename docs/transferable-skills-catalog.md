# Transferable Skills Catalog
## For Designing Learning Extension Trajectories (LxTs)

### About This Catalog

This catalog is a companion to the
[LxT Transferable Skills Worksheet](../templates/worksheets/lxt-transferable-skills-worksheet.md).
It gives you eight ready-to-use **skill cards**. Each card describes a skill in
language that works for any subject, so you can pick one and design an LxT
around it without needing a background in that skill.

Use the cards to:
- Choose the skill your trajectory will target (Worksheet Part 3)
- Find the **observable learner moves** to build into your task (Part 6)
- Borrow **task starters** and adapt them to your subject (Part 6)
- Write a skill reflection prompt (Part 7)

### Transferable Skill vs. Content Objective

| | Content objective | Transferable skill |
|---|---|---|
| Answers | *What* should learners know or do in this subject? | *How* should learners think or work, in any setting? |
| Example | "Explain the causes of inflation" | "Break a complex problem into smaller, solvable parts" |
| Lifespan | Tied to this course | Carried into other courses, jobs and life |
| Where it lives in LCM | LeDs and LbDs | Practised through LxT tasks; revisited in LbDs and LxIs |

A transferable skill is not a new topic to teach. It is a *way of working* that
your learners practise **while** they engage with your subject's content. Your
LxT task is where they practise it on purpose.

### Link to Cognitive Levels

The LbD constructor uses cognitive levels (Recall, Understand, Apply, Analyze,
Evaluate, Create). Transferable skills mostly sit at **Apply and above**. That
is why the LxT **task** (Step C) carries the skill, while the **assimilation
quiz** (Step D) stays at Recall-Understand level and only checks that the
learner has accessed the resource.

### How to Read a Skill Card

Every card has the same seven parts:

1. **In plain words** - a definition you can share with learners as is
2. **Why it matters after college** - the situations where graduates need it
3. **Observable learner moves** - what you would *see* a learner doing
4. **Progression** - Emerging, Developing and Proficient levels
5. **Task starters** - fill-in sentence stems for your LxT task
6. **What it looks like in...** - three short examples from different subjects
7. **Design pitfalls** - common mistakes when designing for this skill

Text in `[square brackets]` is a placeholder. Replace it with something from
your own subject.

## Skill 1: Problem Decomposition

**In plain words:** Breaking a large, messy problem into smaller parts that can
each be understood or solved, and working out how the parts fit back together.

**Why it matters after college:** Workplace problems rarely arrive neatly
packaged. Graduates must turn vague goals ("improve this", "fix that") into a
plan of manageable steps.

**Observable learner moves:**
- Lists the sub-problems or components of a larger problem
- Identifies which parts depend on others, and what to tackle first
- Separates what is known from what still needs to be found out
- Recombines partial solutions and checks the whole still makes sense

**Progression:**

| Emerging | Developing | Proficient |
|---|---|---|
| Treats the problem as one block, or splits it in an arbitrary way | Splits the problem into sensible parts but misses links between them | Splits the problem into parts, orders them by dependency and checks the recombined result |

**Task starters:**
- "Break `[a problem in your subject]` into 3-5 smaller questions. Which one
  must be answered first, and why?"
- "In `[the resource]`, the expert solves `[problem]`. List the steps they took
  as separate sub-problems."
- "Which part of `[problem]` could you solve today, and which parts need more
  information?"

**What it looks like in...**
- *STEM:* Splitting the design of a water filter into flow rate, filtering
  material, cost and maintenance.
- *Humanities / social science:* Breaking "Why did this movement succeed?" into
  leadership, timing, public support and opposition.
- *Professional / commerce:* Turning "Increase shop sales" into footfall,
  conversion, basket size and repeat visits.

**Design pitfalls:**
- Giving learners the sub-problems already listed (that removes the skill)
- Choosing a problem so small it has nothing to decompose

## Skill 2: Systems Thinking

**In plain words:** Seeing how the parts of a situation are connected, how a
change in one part ripples through the others, and how the whole behaves
differently from any single part.

**Why it matters after college:** Decisions at work and in society have side
effects. Graduates who see connections can anticipate consequences instead of
being surprised by them.

**Observable learner moves:**
- Identifies the parts (actors, elements) of a system and how they connect
- Traces cause-and-effect chains, including feedback loops
- Predicts side effects or delayed effects of a change
- Identifies where a small change could have a large effect

**Progression:**

| Emerging | Developing | Proficient |
|---|---|---|
| Sees one cause leading to one effect | Maps several connections, but treats them as one-way chains | Identifies feedback loops and delays, and predicts knock-on effects |

**Task starters:**
- "Draw the connections between `[4-6 elements in your subject]`. Mark one
  place where an effect feeds back to its cause."
- "If `[one element]` changes, what else changes? What changes *later*?"
- "In `[the resource]`, find one consequence nobody planned for. What
  connection caused it?"

**What it looks like in...**
- *STEM:* Explaining how removing one species changes the rest of a food web.
- *Humanities / social science:* Tracing how a new law changed prices, jobs and
  migration in a region.
- *Professional / commerce:* Seeing how a discount boosts sales now but may
  reduce profit and change customer expectations later.

**Design pitfalls:**
- Asking only for a list of parts, with no connections between them
- Using examples with a single obvious cause and effect

## Skill 3: Abstraction and Pattern Recognition

**In plain words:** Spotting what different situations have in common, ignoring
details that do not matter, and forming a general idea or model that can be
reused.

**Why it matters after college:** Graduates meet problems they have never seen.
Recognising "this is like that other problem" lets them reuse what they already
know.

**Observable learner moves:**
- Compares two or more cases and names what they share
- Separates essential features from incidental details
- States a general rule, model or template from specific cases
- Applies the general idea to a new, unfamiliar case

**Progression:**

| Emerging | Developing | Proficient |
|---|---|---|
| Describes each case separately | Notices surface similarities between cases | Names the underlying structure and uses it on a new case |

**Task starters:**
- "`[Resource]` shows two cases of `[phenomenon]`. What do they have in common
  underneath the surface details?"
- "Write one general rule that would explain both `[case A]` and `[case B]`."
- "Where else, outside this course, might the same pattern appear?"

**What it looks like in...**
- *STEM:* Recognising that population growth and compound interest follow the
  same mathematical pattern.
- *Humanities / social science:* Seeing a common "rise, crisis, reform" pattern
  across two different historical periods.
- *Professional / commerce:* Using one customer complaint process as a template
  for handling supplier complaints.

**Design pitfalls:**
- Offering only one case (patterns need at least two)
- Naming the pattern for learners before they look for it

## Skill 4: Evaluating Evidence and Sources

**In plain words:** Judging how trustworthy, relevant and complete a piece of
information is before using it to reach a conclusion.

**Why it matters after college:** Graduates are flooded with information,
claims and AI-generated content. Deciding what to trust is a daily task at work
and as a citizen.

**Observable learner moves:**
- Asks who produced the information, when, and why
- Separates claims from evidence that supports them
- Checks one source against another
- States how confident they are in a conclusion, and why

**Progression:**

| Emerging | Developing | Proficient |
|---|---|---|
| Accepts information at face value | Questions the source but does not weigh the evidence | Weighs evidence from several sources and states their level of confidence |

**Task starters:**
- "`[Resource]` makes the claim `[claim]`. What evidence does it give? What
  evidence is missing?"
- "Find one other source on `[topic]`. Where do the two agree and disagree?"
- "How confident are you in `[conclusion]`: low, medium or high? Why?"

**What it looks like in...**
- *STEM:* Judging whether a study's sample size supports its headline claim.
- *Humanities / social science:* Comparing a government report and a newspaper
  account of the same event.
- *Professional / commerce:* Checking a supplier's marketing claims against
  independent reviews.

**Design pitfalls:**
- Using only sources that are obviously reliable or obviously fake
- Asking "Is this source reliable?" without asking *why*

## Skill 5: Communicating for Different Audiences

**In plain words:** Explaining the same idea in different ways, depending on who
is listening and what they need from you.

**Why it matters after college:** Graduates must explain their work to
managers, clients, the public and colleagues from other fields, often in very
little time.

**Observable learner moves:**
- Identifies the audience and what it already knows and needs
- Chooses suitable words, examples and format for that audience
- Removes or explains specialist terms
- Checks whether the message was understood

**Progression:**

| Emerging | Developing | Proficient |
|---|---|---|
| Uses one style for every audience | Adjusts vocabulary but keeps the same structure | Changes vocabulary, examples, format and length to suit each audience |

**Task starters:**
- "Explain the key idea from `[resource]` in 3 sentences to `[a school
  student / a client / a policymaker]`."
- "Rewrite this paragraph from `[resource]` for someone with no background in
  `[your subject]`."
- "What one question would `[audience]` most likely ask? Answer it."

**What it looks like in...**
- *STEM:* Explaining a lab result to a fellow scientist and to a patient.
- *Humanities / social science:* Turning a research finding into a short public
  notice.
- *Professional / commerce:* Presenting the same budget to a manager and to a
  shop-floor team.

**Design pitfalls:**
- Not naming a specific audience
- Judging only correctness, not fit to the audience

## Skill 6: Collaboration

**In plain words:** Working with others towards a shared goal: dividing work,
listening, building on others' ideas and resolving disagreements.

**Why it matters after college:** Almost all work happens in teams, often
across disciplines, locations and cultures.

**Observable learner moves:**
- Agrees on a shared goal and divides roles
- Builds on or questions others' contributions respectfully
- Integrates different viewpoints into one product
- Reflects on how the team worked, not just what it produced

**Progression:**

| Emerging | Developing | Proficient |
|---|---|---|
| Works alongside others but separately | Shares work and listens, but avoids disagreement | Combines different viewpoints and works through disagreement productively |

**Task starters:**
- "With a peer, compare your notes on `[resource]`. Agree on the 3 most
  important points and record one point where you disagreed."
- "Each team member reads a different part of `[resource]`. Combine your
  findings into one summary."
- "What did your partner see in `[resource]` that you missed?"

**What it looks like in...**
- *STEM:* Pairs dividing a dataset and merging their analyses.
- *Humanities / social science:* A group combining different interpretations of
  the same text into one position.
- *Professional / commerce:* A team agreeing on one marketing plan from
  competing proposals.

**Design pitfalls:**
- Group tasks that one person can complete alone
- Assessing only the final product, never the process

**Note for MOOCs:** Collaboration in an LxT is often asynchronous. Use the
course discussion forum or peer review features, and connect the task to an
LxI.

## Skill 7: Ethical Reasoning

**In plain words:** Recognising when a decision affects people's rights,
wellbeing or fairness, weighing different viewpoints, and justifying a choice.

**Why it matters after college:** Every profession faces decisions where the
technically correct answer is not the only consideration.

**Observable learner moves:**
- Identifies who is affected by a decision (stakeholders)
- Recognises the values or principles in tension
- Considers at least two viewpoints fairly
- Justifies a position and acknowledges its costs

**Progression:**

| Emerging | Developing | Proficient |
|---|---|---|
| Sees a right/wrong answer only | Names stakeholders and viewpoints | Weighs competing values and justifies a position, acknowledging trade-offs |

**Task starters:**
- "Who benefits and who bears the cost in `[situation from resource]`?"
- "Argue for `[option A]`, then for `[option B]`. Which do you choose, and what
  do you give up?"
- "What would a `[stakeholder]` say about this decision?"

**What it looks like in...**
- *STEM:* Weighing the benefits of a new technology against privacy concerns.
- *Humanities / social science:* Judging a historical leader's decision against
  the values of their time and of ours.
- *Professional / commerce:* Deciding whether to cut costs by switching to a
  cheaper supplier with poor labour practices.

**Design pitfalls:**
- Cases with one obvious "good" answer
- Marking learners on which side they choose instead of how they reason

## Skill 8: Self-Directed Learning (Metacognition)

**In plain words:** Knowing how you learn: setting your own learning goals,
finding resources, checking your understanding and changing approach when
something is not working.

**Why it matters after college:** Knowledge and tools change quickly. Graduates
must keep learning without a teacher or a syllabus.

**Observable learner moves:**
- Sets a specific learning goal before engaging with a resource
- Monitors understanding while learning ("Do I get this?")
- Chooses a strategy or resource that suits the goal
- Reviews what worked and plans what to do next

**Progression:**

| Emerging | Developing | Proficient |
|---|---|---|
| Follows instructions without a personal goal | Sets goals but rarely checks progress | Sets goals, monitors progress and adjusts strategy |

**Task starters:**
- "Before opening `[resource]`, write one question you want it to answer.
  Afterwards: did it?"
- "Choose one of these three resources on `[topic]`. Why did you choose it?"
- "What was the hardest part of `[resource]`? What did you do when you got
  stuck?"

**What it looks like in...**
- *STEM:* A learner deciding whether a video or a worked example will help them
  more with a difficult concept.
- *Humanities / social science:* A learner planning their own reading path on a
  topic and reviewing it.
- *Professional / commerce:* A learner picking a free online tutorial to learn
  a new spreadsheet function needed for a task.

**Design pitfalls:**
- Prescribing every step, so learners never make choices
- Reflection prompts that ask "Did you like it?" instead of "How did you learn?"
